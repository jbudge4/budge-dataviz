LAY READER VERSION

Do Smaller Animal Really Sleep More?

Some mammals spend much of their day asleep, while others get by on only a few hours. Why such a difference? Scientists have proposed several benefits of sleep, including saving energy and supporting learning and memory. But how long an animal sleeps may also depend on how much time it needs to find food and how safely it can rest without encountering predators (Capellini et al., 2008). Could an animal’s size help explain its sleeping habits?

We explored this question using sleep and body-weight records for 83 mammal species from the msleep dataset. These measurements come from data compiled by Savage and West (2007). Each dot in Figure 1 represents one species, showing its weight in kilograms and how many hours it sleeps per day.

To fit tiny and enormous mammals on the same graph, the weight axis increases in multiples of ten: from 0.01 kg to 0.1 kg, then 1 kg, 10 kg, and so on. We added a straight line to summarize the overall trend.

Code 
# Visualization 1: body size and daily sleep in mammals
# Data: ggplot2::msleep
# Source: https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/reference/msleep.html
# Sleep and weight data: Savage & West (2007), doi:10.1073/pnas.0610080104


#calling msleep function from package
library(ggplot2)

sleep_data <- ggplot2::msleep

sleep_plot_1 <- ggplot(sleep_data, aes(x = bodywt, y = sleep_total)) +
  # Draw the teal line and confidence band behind the points.
  geom_smooth(
    method = "lm",
    formula = y ~ x,
    se = TRUE,
    level = 0.95,
    colour = "#007C83",
    fill = "#007C83",
    alpha = 0.15
  ) +
  # Match the points in the second plot.
  geom_point(size = 2.8, alpha = 0.75, colour = "grey30") +

  # Each labelled step represents a tenfold increase in weight.
  scale_x_log10(
    breaks = c(0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000),
    labels = c("0.01", "0.1", "1", "10", "100", "1,000")
  ) +
  scale_y_continuous(breaks = seq(0, 24, 4)) +
  coord_cartesian(
    xlim = range(sleep_data$bodywt),
    ylim = c(0, 24)
  ) +

  labs(
    title = "Mammal Sleep (hrs) vs Body Size (kg)",
    x = "Body weight (kg)",
    y = "Hours of sleep per day" ) +

  # Match the second plot's theme and text sizes.
  theme_minimal(base_size = 14) +
  theme(
    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),
    panel.grid.major.x = element_blank(),
    plot.title = element_text(face = "bold", size = 20),
    plot.subtitle = element_text(size = 12),
    plot.caption = element_text(hjust = 0, size = 10)
  )

print(sleep_plot_1)

The first graph reveals a potentially surprising pattern: smaller mammals tend to sleep longer than larger mammals. The shaded band around the line shows uncertainty about the estimated average sleep duration at each body weight.

But this broad comparison brings together very different animals. Does the pattern hold when we compare species that are more closely related?

To take a closer look, we split the data into groups of related mammals. We included only groups with at least six species in the dataset, avoiding comparisons based on just a handful of points. This left four groups: rodents such as mice and squirrels (22 species); cats, dogs, seals and their relatives (12 species); primates, including monkeys, apes and humans (12 species); and cattle, deer, pigs and their relatives (6 species).

Together, these groups include 52 of the original 83 species. Each panel in Figure 2 has its own trend line, but all use the same scales so they can be compared directly.

    panel.grid.major.x = element_blank(),

    plot.title = element_text(face = "bold", size = 18),

The closer look reveals a less consistent pattern. Among rodents and the group containing cats, dogs and seals, the lines are almost flat: larger species do not appear to sleep much more or less than smaller ones. Among primates, however, larger species still tend to sleep less. The line for cattle, deer, pigs and their relatives also slopes downward, but with only six species and a wide shaded band, that trend is uncertain.

The overall pattern therefore tells only part of the story. Smaller mammals generally sleep longer in this dataset, but the relationship becomes less consistent when we compare more closely related species. Some of the broad pattern may reflect which groups of animals we are comparing. These graphs help reveal that possibility, although they cannot tell us what causes the differences or whether the differences between the groups’ trends are more than we might expect by chance.

SCIENCE VERSION

Body Mass and Sleep Duration Across and Within Mammalian Orders

Sleep duration varies considerably across mammalian species. Proposed physiological functions of sleep include conserving energy and supporting learning and memory. However, the benefits of sleep do not necessarily explain why some species sleep longer than others. Ecological demands, such as time spent finding food and exposure to predators while resting, may also constrain sleep duration (Capellini et al., 2008). This raises the question of whether body mass has a consistent association with sleep duration across different groups of mammals.

To examine this relationship, we used the msleep dataset from the ggplot2 package, which contains observations for 83 mammalian species. The analysis focused on body mass in kilograms and total daily sleep duration in hours. The dataset’s sleep and body-mass measurements were compiled from Savage and West (2007). Because body mass varies widely across species, we applied a base-10 logarithmic transformation. This allows very small and very large mammals to be displayed on the same axis, with equal spacing representing tenfold increases in body mass. We fitted a linear regression predicting daily sleep duration from log-transformed body mass.

Code 
# Visualization 1: body size and daily sleep in mammals
# Data: ggplot2::msleep
# Source: https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/reference/msleep.html
# Sleep and weight data: Savage & West (2007), doi:10.1073/pnas.0610080104

#install.packages(ggplot2)

library(ggplot2)

#calling msleep function from package
sleep_data <- ggplot2::msleep

# Create the scatterplot and add a fitted regression line.
sleep_plot_1 <- ggplot(sleep_data, aes(x = bodywt, y = sleep_total)) +
  #scatterplot for each row of data
  geom_point(size = 2.5, alpha = 0.7, colour = "grey30") +
  #linear regression line
  geom_smooth(
    method = "lm",
    formula = y ~ x,
    se = TRUE,
    colour = "black",
    fill = "grey80"
  ) +
  #body weight on a log scale as too much variance in weight normally
  #regression will still plot this even though it comes after
  scale_x_log10(
    breaks = c(0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000, 10000),
    labels = c("0.01", "0.1", "1", "10", "100", "1,000", "10,000")
  ) +
  #y scale
  #set continuous with break point 0-24, 4
  scale_y_continuous(breaks = c(0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24)) +
  #range
  coord_cartesian(ylim = c(0, 24)) +
  #add labels to full plot
  labs(
    title = "Total Mammal Sleep (hrs) vs Body Weight (kgs)",
        x = "Body weight (kg; log scale)",
    y = "Total sleep (hours per day)",
    caption = paste(
      "Line: linear regression; shaded band: 95% confidence interval.",
      "Source: ggplot2::msleep (Savage & West, 2007).",
      sep = "\n"
    )
  ) +
  
  theme_classic(base_size = 12)

print(sleep_plot_1)

Figure 1 shows a negative association across the 83 species: smaller mammals generally sleep longer than larger mammals. The fitted line summarizes this relationship, while the shaded band represents a 95% confidence interval around the estimated mean sleep duration. Although this overall pattern may seem surprising, it could partly reflect differences between groups of mammals. Does the same negative association appear when we compare species within groups of more closely related mammals?

To explore whether the overall relationship also appears within groups of related mammals, we repeated the plot separately for each mammalian order with at least six observations. This selection retained four orders: Rodentia (rodents; 22 species), Carnivora (carnivorans; 12 species), Primates (12 species), and Artiodactyla (even-toed ungulates; 6 species). The six-observation threshold was a practical choice to exclude groups with very few species. Together, these orders account for 52 of the original 83 observations. We fitted a separate regression line for each order, using the same log-transformed body mass and axis ranges as in Figure 1.

Figure 2 shows that the overall negative association is not consistent across these groups. Among rodents and carnivorans, the fitted lines are relatively flat, suggesting little association between body mass and sleep duration within these samples. Primates show a downward relationship, with larger species generally sleeping less. The fitted line for even-toed ungulates also slopes downward, but the small sample and wide confidence band make this estimate uncertain.

These patterns suggest that the overall association may partly reflect differences between groups of mammals. However, the plots alone do not establish that the groups’ relationships differ statistically or explain what causes the differences in sleep duration.
